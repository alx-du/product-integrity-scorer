## Abstract

The United Nations Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights establish a corporate responsibility to prevent and account for adverse human rights impacts across value chains, including those arising through environmental harm.[^1] Fifteen years on, the Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights notes that accountability gaps persist.[^2]

Such gaps are addressed through several mechanisms; this study examines a precondition of one: demand-side pressure at the point of purchase. Labor rights violations cut production costs; certification without independent verification lets that saving hide behind a claim; and the premium paid for the certified good flows to the concealing firm rather than to remedy. Such pressure depends on evidence reaching the terminal node of the chain, and research makes that evidence necessary but not sufficient: disclosing concealment reduces intent toward the concealing actor, while price and availability still dominate what people buy. The study therefore asks not whether disclosure changes conduct, but whether the evidence it depends on is available at all.

Two propositions are tested: that the gap is one of distribution rather than absence, and that no single source class covers every dimension due diligence requires. Both hold. An audit of six goods found no assessor covering more than three of five dimensions, and no evidenced claim on the environmental or labor dimension for any good. A retrieval against commercial supply-chain intelligence was admissible for two dimensions and rejected for three: the inputs are largely public, but the entity resolution needed to use them is not.

The precondition is not met. A third proposition follows without separate test: any composite blends dimensions that are backed by entirely different bodies of evidence. The study specifies a non-compensatory architecture accordingly, and a rule for disclosing compliance-grade evidence to non-expert readers without implying harm it does not establish. Whether meeting the precondition delivers remedy is not tested here.

**Keywords:** business and human rights; UNGPs; human rights due diligence; forced labor; value chain transparency; composite indicators; non-compensatory aggregation; information asymmetry

## 1. Aim, Problem, and Research Questions

### 1.1 The problem

The Guiding Principles place on business enterprises a responsibility to conduct human rights due diligence across their operations, subsidiaries, and business relationships, including impacts to which they are directly linked even where they did not cause or contribute to them.[^1] That responsibility assumes knowledge. Knowledge is what's scarce when a chain runs across many jurisdictions, tiers, and legal entities, and it is not scarce for everyone equally. Companies, compliance intelligence providers, customs authorities, and enforcement agencies hold a great deal of resolved information about who supplies whom and where labor risk sits. At the end of the chain, where the purchase that pays for all of it happens, almost none of that is available.

This is usually called a market imperfection. It is better understood as a governance problem: the moment money changes hands is the moment the evidence is thinnest.

### 1.2 Objectives, propositions, and long-term aim

Practice-based research often runs three things together: what a study tests, what it proposes, and what the wider program is trying to achieve. Only the first two are claims of this paper.

#### Research objectives

1. To establish which dimensions of corporate conduct are evidenceable at the level of the individual traded good from publicly and commercially available sources.
2. To specify an assessment architecture that reports those dimensions independently, without aggregation.
3. To derive the conditions under which evidence produced for compliance analysis may be disclosed to non-expert audiences without generating unfounded attribution of harm.

#### Propositions

Two propositions are tested against evidence generated by the study:

- **P1 (distributional deficit).** The evidence needed for good-level due diligence is unevenly distributed, not missing. Its raw inputs are largely public. What is proprietary is the entity resolution that makes them usable.
- **P2 (non-spanning).** No single source class covers all five dimensions material to due diligence at the level of the individual traded good.

A third proposition follows from P2 without a separate test, and is marked as derived throughout:

- **P3 (aggregation, derived).** Each dimension rests on different sources of different quality. A composite rolls them into one number and hides the unevenness.

#### Long-term program aim, stated and not tested

This study belongs to a longer program aiming to make goods that clear a threshold on every dimension both cheaper and easier to find. The reasoning is that price and availability follow from production volume, so they are outcomes rather than fixed limits. When §2.5a identifies price and availability as the main obstacles, it is describing current conditions instead of permanent ones.

It is important to clarify that **this aim is not a claim of this research**, as it is not tested here, and §6.3 sets out the untested causal chain it depends on. In addition, "qualifying" carries the upstream meaning set out in §3.9. A qualifying good is not one that gives the purchaser a good deal. It is one whose price supports a living wage upstream, and which clears its threshold on the other four dimensions as well.

#### The relation between the aim and this study

The program's aim requires identifying goods that meet a threshold on every dimension. That requires evidence in every dimension. This study tests whether such evidence is available at the level of the individual traded good, and finds that it is not. The precondition for the program is unmet, and showing as much is what this study contributes.

### 1.3 End-user, and the distinction from rights-holders

The **primary end-user** is the demand-side actor exercising a purchase decision under conditions of evidentiary scarcity: an actor already disposed to attend, but without research access to verify claims. The **secondary end-user** is the producer or brand whose responsible practices remain invisible to buyers. The established route to visibility is certification, but §2.3 and §2.4 document why that route has degraded: marks are obtainable without meaningful verification, so buyers have reason to distrust them, and a producer who genuinely earns one gets little credit for it.

However, **rights-holders are NOT an end-user** in this case. The framework does not serve workers and communities whose rights are or may be adversely affected by the activities under assessment directly. Its service to rights-holders is indirect and contingent, as it may alter the information environment in which pressure is exerted on duty-bearers. The question of whether better information produces remedy is one this study does not answer, and §6.3 states that boundary.

## 2. Context and Literature

### 2.1 The normative frame

The Human Rights Council unanimously endorsed the Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights in resolution 17/4 of June 16, 2011.[^1] Their architecture is three-parted: the State duty to protect against human rights abuse by third parties, the corporate responsibility to respect human rights, and access to effective remedy for those affected.[^4] The corporate responsibility extends beyond avoiding harm the enterprise causes, to preventing or mitigating adverse impacts directly linked to its operations, products, or services through business relationships, even where the enterprise has not contributed to the impact.[^5] Operationalizing that responsibility requires human rights due diligence: assessing impacts, integrating findings, tracking responses, and communicating them publicly.[^5]

Environmental harm was eventually recognized as a right in its own right, ten years after the Guiding Principles were adopted, in two steps: the Human Rights Council affirmed the right to a clean, healthy and sustainable environment in resolution 48/13 (2021), and the General Assembly followed with resolution 76/300 (2022). The Assembly resolution calls on business enterprises to give effect to the right via adopting policies. Thus, environmental impact is treated in this study as a rights impact, which is what allows Planet and People & Supply Chain to sit inside one framework.

Marking 15 years since endorsement, OHCHR observes that while the Guiding Principles have been transformative, including in judicial decisions clarifying standards on corporate due diligence and access to remedy, accountability gaps and barriers to remedy still persist.[^2] Furthermore, when a violation is hidden instead of remedied, and hiding it pays, the remedy pillar is not just unmet. It is working backwards.

### 2.2 The scale and trajectory of the harm

The ILO, Walk Free and IOM estimate 27.6 million persons in forced labor on any given day in the 2017–2021 reference period, within a modern slavery population of approximately 49.6 million.[^7] 68% of forced labor occurs in the private economy, and the ILO estimates annual illegal profits from forced labor at USD 236 billion, a measure of value taken from workers and kept inside the chain.[^8]

On child labor, ILO and UNICEF estimate 138 million children in child labor in 2024, including approximately 54 million in hazardous work.[^9] The 2020–2024 period reversed the deterioration recorded between 2016 and 2020, but the world missed the Sustainable Development Goal Target 8.7 deadline to end child labor by 2025.[^9] Eliminating child labor by 2030 would require an annual rate of reduction more than 11 times faster than that achieved since 2020; achieving it by 2045 would require a sevenfold acceleration.[^10]

The environmental estimates are built differently. UNEP's International Resource Panel reports that material extraction has tripled over the past five decades, rising from 30 to 106 billion tonnes annually since 1970, and that extraction and processing of material resources account for more than half of global greenhouse gas emissions and for 40% of health-related impacts of air pollution.[^11] Extraction is projected to rise by 60% from 2020 levels by 2060 on current trends.[^11] High-income countries use six times more materials per person and generate ten times the climate impact of low-income countries.[^11]

The estimates above measure the scale of the harm. The closest public instrument to identifying *which goods* are implicated is the US Department of Labor's TVPRA List, which pairs 204 goods with 82 countries and areas where ILAB has documented evidence of production with child or forced labor.[^12]

### 2.3 The mechanism: violation as cost reduction

**First, rights violations cut production costs by pushing them onto other people.** Forced labor, unpaid overtime, suppressed organizing, and hazardous conditions are a means of achieving margin, and the ILO's USD 236 billion estimate measures that cost reduction realized as profit.[^8] Unabated emissions, untreated discharge, and unremediated extraction do the same: transferring cost to the communities and ecosystems that bear the impact.[^11]

**Second, cheaper production wins market share.** US authorities have framed forced-labor import restriction partly in terms of the unfair advantage gained by producers relying on it.[^13]

**Third, certification without inspection permits concealment.** Where a scheme's verification is weak or absent, the claim it confers becomes separated from the conduct it attests. European Commission analysis finds that roughly half of green labels in the EU offer weak or non-existent verification.[^14]

**Fourth, the premium rewards concealment.** Someone paying above market for a certified good, where verification is weak, is transferring money to whoever made the concealment work.

Voluntary certification is often described as how firms meet the responsibility to respect human rights. Without verification, firms are buying the appearance of having met it. By soaking up the willingness to pay that might have funded remedy, weak certification can block the third pillar instead of advancing it.

### 2.4 Certification as a governance instrument, and its verification deficit

Sustainability and labor attributes are credence goods: unlike price or appearance, they cannot be verified by the buyer before, during, or after purchase.[^15] Certification addresses this by interposing a third party between the claim and the buyer. The mechanism requires a substantive standard, independent verification, and a mark the buyer can distinguish from unverified claims. All three conditions are under strain.

Ecolabel Index tracks 456 schemes across 199 countries and 25 industry sectors.[^16] The European Commission identified 230 sustainability labels and 100 green energy labels in the EU alone.[^14] In a November 2020 sweep, EU consumer protection authorities concluded that 42% of examined environmental claims were likely exaggerated, false or deceptive.[^17] Of 344 claims examined in detail, authorities could not judge accuracy in 57.5% of cases, could not determine which lifecycle stage the claim covered in 75%, and could not determine whether it applied to the whole good or a single component in 50%.[^18] The problem is less about dishonesty than about scope: nobody can tell what the claim covers, and a claim whose subject is undefined cannot support due diligence.

On the labor side, the problem runs deeper. LeBaron, Lister and Dauvergne argue from field research that the ethical audit regime protects industry commercial interests and absorbs pressure for binding state regulation, functioning as part of the industry-led privatization of governance rather than the neutral role it is credited with.[^19]

In summary, environmental claims fail because verification is weak or the subject is unclear. Labor audits fail because the tool was not built to detect. Both leave the §2.3 mechanism intact: the premium stays attached to the claim, not the conduct.

### 2.5 Two governance trajectories, opposite directions

Two major markets responded to the concealment problem in §2.3 with opposite approaches, and both leave this study's problem untouched.

**The EU chose disclosure: require firms to report, and let pressure follow.** The Green Claims Directive (proposed 2023) would have required independent verification of environmental claims before they could be made; it stalled in June 2025 when the Commission moved to withdraw it.[^20][^21] The weaker Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition Directive (Directive (EU) 2024/825) survives and applies from September 2026.[^22] The Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (Directive (EU) 2024/1760), the first binding translation of the Guiding Principles' second pillar in a major market, was narrowed by Directive (EU) 2026/470: scope, liability, and penalties were reduced, and first application deferred to July 2029.[^23]

**The US chose exclusion: bar goods at the border and make the importer prove they are clean.** DHS added 43 companies to the UFLPA Entity List on July 31, 2026, bringing it to 187.[^24] Goods from listed entities carry a rebuttable presumption of forced-labor production that the importer must overcome by clear and convincing evidence.[^13] Section 301 forced-labor tariffs took effect against 60 economies on July 24, 2026.[^13]

The disclosure model retreated while the exclusion model is intensifying. But they fail in the same way for this study's purposes: disclosure assumes information will reach people who apply pressure, and it now produces less of it; exclusion works at the border and produces nothing a buyer can see. Neither closes the gap at the point of purchase.

### 2.5a The behavioral precondition

Where regulation is absent or delayed, demand-side pressure is the usual fallback. Information is necessary for it to work but not enough: people move away from a firm once its concealment is exposed, yet price and convenience still decide most of what they buy.

Zimand-Sheiner and Lissitsa surveyed 607 fast-fashion consumers before and after exposure to negative environmental information about the brand. Negative attitudes and perceived betrayal correlated with reduced purchase intentions.[^25] Disclosing concealment moves stated intention, but stated intention translates poorly into action. Potter and colleagues tested this with actual choices: two randomized trials in an experimental online supermarket found environmental impact labels reduced basket environmental impact by only approximately 2.0 to 2.3%.[^26] Small but significant, and measured on selections rather than intentions.

The gap between intention and action is well documented. German household panel data shows that sustainability-positive households cover only 14.6% of their fast-moving goods demand with sustainable products, with financial and practical barriers as the main obstacle.[^27] A UK survey of 1,500 younger consumers found 59% conceding their generation talks about sustainability more than it practices it; commitment was lower among the youngest respondents than among older ones in the same cohort.[^28] Ultra-fast fashion purchase rates among self-identified environmentally oriented younger consumers are near-identical to the cohort as a whole.[^29] Price, availability, and inertia dominate, consistent with earlier findings.[^30][^31]

Three implications follow. This research addresses the precondition for demand-side pressure, not the mechanism, because information is necessary but not sufficient. The evidence for negative information reducing intent is stronger than for positive information raising willingness to pay, and the instrument built here surfaces gaps and unverified claims rather than certifying good performance, so it should behave differently from the labeling studies tested by Potter. **That is a reason to test, not a finding.** While price and availability are identified above as the main obstacles, this describes current conditions, not permanent ones; both follow from production volume, which is what supports the long-term aim in §1.2 and supports no causal claim in this paper.

### 2.6 The economic literature, subordinated

Akerlof's account of quality uncertainty establishes the market mechanism: where buyers cannot observe quality, they discount all claims, and higher-quality producers exit.[^15] Labor and environmental conditions are a severe case, since they remain unobservable even after purchase. Akerlof explains why a market underpays for quality nobody can see, but does not explain why the harm lands on people outside the transaction. The economics describes how cost moves; the Guiding Principles identify who carries it.

### 2.7 Three gaps in the literature

**(a) Level of analysis.** Existing research asks whether certification schemes are credible and whether claims are accurate. Little of it asks what evidence is available at all for a single traded good, dimension by dimension. This study measures availability, not credibility.

**(b) Non-aggregation.** The composite-indicator methodology literature is well developed, but its application to good-level rights and environmental assessment is thin. Non-aggregation is generally treated as a failure to aggregate rather than a defensible position.

**(c) Distribution, not absence.** The literature treats the gap as though the evidence does not exist. However, corporate ownership is public from securities filings; US bills of lading are public; sanctions designations, the UFLPA Entity List, and the TVPRA List are freely downloadable, as are the academic reports behind most commercial forced-labor indicators. What is not free is the join: narrowing hundreds of name matches to one legal entity and working back through tiers 2, 3, and 4 across jurisdictions and languages. The evidence is public; the resolution is not — commercial providers hold the resolved data and sell it to compliance teams at institutional prices, which shows who can reach it, not whether it exists.

## 3. Method

### 3.1 Framework specification

#### The five dimensions

The framework assesses a traded good across five dimensions, held constant across all product categories:

**Planet.** Environmental impact across sourcing, land use, material throughput, packaging, and emissions. Per §2.1, this is a rights dimension working through the right to a clean, healthy and sustainable environment.[^6]

**People & Supply Chain.** Labor conditions in production, and whether the origin of materials is disclosed at a level permitting assessment.

**Quality.** How the buyer's specification and price pressure shape conditions upstream. Durability matters as a denominator, not as a virtue in itself. The evidentiary basis for treating this as a rights-relevant construct is set out in §3.9.

**Value.** The distribution of value along the chain, assessed at two points. Upstream: whether the price paid to producers is consistent with a living wage. Downstream: what share of the price goes to production inputs and craftsmanship versus brand rent and marketing. Both are questions about where value accrues, not about whether the purchaser transacted favorably. The evidentiary basis is set out in §3.9.

**Transparency.** Whether the claims made about a good rest on evidence someone outside the company can check, and how specific that evidence is.

#### Fixed dimensions, variable indicators

The five dimensions never change. The indicators inside them are chosen per product category, as the risks attached to an egg, a smart lock, and a shirt differ in kind, not just in degree. One indicator set across all three would either miss what matters or measure what does not apply.

Fixed dimensions keep goods comparable across categories; variable indicators keep the measures relevant to each. Two goods from different categories can be compared on whether each clears its People & Supply Chain threshold, not on the evidence that got them there.

Choosing indicators is also where the framework is most open to manipulation, since a scheme that picks its own measures can be tuned toward a desired answer. That choice is treated as part of the specification, and §3.6 documents it for the goods assessed here.

#### Three axes, reported independently

Each dimension yields three outputs, and folding these into one number would discard distinctions the rest of the paper needs.

1. **Score.** A 0 to 5 assessment of the indicators populating that dimension, where 0 denotes assessed and adverse.
2. **Confidence.** High, Medium, or Low, per §3.3, reflecting the independence of the verifying source rather than the strength of the finding.
3. **Coverage.** Whether the dimension was assessable at all, as one of four display states: Scored, Inferred, Claimed, or Unknown.

#### Absence is not a score

**A dimension with no evidence does not score zero.** It returns Unknown, which is not a low point on the scale but not a point on the scale at all. A zero means evidence existed, someone examined it, and the good performs badly; Unknown means there was nothing to examine. Under the Guiding Principles these are separate failures: a bad finding points to conduct requiring prevention or mitigation, while an absence points to a communication failure under Principle 21, where a firm has not given anyone enough information to judge its response.[^5] A framework that cannot tell the two apart cannot support due diligence.

**Neither Unknown nor Claimed carries a numeric value**, so no average across dimensions can be calculated when either appears. Since both rights-bearing dimensions sit in one such state for every good assessed (Table 1), the composite is unavailable across the whole sample whether or not a reader accepts the argument of principle against it. And because Unknown is a non-score, a good with an Unknown dimension cannot clear a threshold, so it fails the conjunctive screen without anyone having judged its performance.

#### Aggregation within dimensions

Indicators combine within a dimension but never across dimensions (§3.5), as indicators within a dimension measure facets of the same thing and can reasonably substitute for or combine with one another, while dimensions themselves should not.

#### When coverage is adequate

The boundaries between the four display states determine how many goods fail on coverage, so they are operational definitions:

- **Scored.** At least one tier 1 or tier 2 source addresses the dimension for the good itself.
- **Inferred.** Evidence at entity or category level reaches the good through a stated assumption, at capped confidence.
- **Claimed.** The only material addressing the dimension comes from the enterprise selling the good, repeated without independent verification.
- **Unknown.** No source addresses the dimension at any level.

Inferred is a coverage state, not a confidence tier: a dimension can be Inferred with High confidence and still not be Scored. Because the boundary is set by source tier rather than volume, a large body of tier 4 material stays Inferred, so quantity cannot substitute for proximity to the good.

Claimed is separated from both Scored and Unknown, as an unverified claim by an interested party is the mechanism of concealment described in §2.3. Counting it as evidence adopts the concealment; counting it as nothing loses the fact that a claim was made. Claimed does not satisfy the conjunctive screen: a dimension supported only by the seller's assertion has not been shown to meet a threshold and is thus disqualified alongside Unknown. The two are kept apart because they call for different responses: Unknown asks an enterprise to disclose; Claimed asks it to substantiate.

#### Scoring within a dimension

The scale runs 0 to 5, with generic anchors across dimensions: 1 very poor through 5 best-in-class with independent verification. Each product category illustrates what those anchors require. A 5 on labor in apparel requires third-party factory certification and a public supplier list; a 5 on ingredient transparency in personal care requires a full INCI list, allergen reporting, and third-party verification. 0 denotes assessed and adverse; absence returns Unknown or Claimed, which carries no number.

Indicators are priority-ordered and combined by gating, not averaging: a dimension score is the highest anchor whose evidentiary requirements are met, not a mean. A strong secondary indicator does not substitute for a missing primary one.

The threshold for a pass is 3.0, the point the scale marks as acceptable. No good in the sample scored high enough on every dimension for this threshold to apply — Unknown and Claimed disqualify before performance is scored. Thus, the findings report a failure of evidence rather than a failure of performance.

Currently, the specification applies one gating logic across all five dimensions. A rights-bearing dimension (Planet, People and Supply Chain) should require independent verification for any passing score, while a commercial one (Quality, Value, Transparency) does not.

### 3.2 Assisted research protocol

The protocol follows the NYU Ethical Tech CoLab workflow, which breaks down assisted research into gap identification, question formulation, summarization, contradiction detection, and integration, with guardrails for each.[^32] Assisted systems were used in two ways.

**First use: construction of this study.** Literature survey, gap surfacing, and testing of question specificity and feasibility, under four guardrail conditions detailed in Appendix D.[^32]

**Second use: structured claim extraction within the evidence pipeline.** The pipeline ingests source material and emits per-dimension claims with evidence typing, confidence tier, gap markers, and attribution. It extracts claims, not ratings, and treats dimensional non-coverage as first-class data.

Both usages assess assertions made by an interested party where the assessor cannot observe the underlying conditions — the same position a due diligence function occupies under the Guiding Principles.[^5] The parallel is developed in Appendix D.

### 3.3 Source credibility and confidence tiering

Three tiers: High (independently verified), Medium (interested-party claim supported by evidence), Low (inferred). No rating is permitted without a source.

This adapts the CoLab's journal-credibility rubric, which scores sources by peer-review rigor.[^32] The CoLab's rubric uses five tiers scaled by peer-review rigor;[^32] three suffice here, as the evidence comes from regulatory filings, customs records, and certification registries, not journals. The operative test is not peer review but whether the verifier is independent of the firm being assessed.

### 3.4 Evidence hierarchy for rights-related claims

A claim about rights carries consequences a claim about quality does not. The hierarchy:

1. **Observed conditions.** Facility audit, worker testimony, labor inspectorate finding, judicial determination.
2. **Official designation.** Sanctions listing, UFLPA Entity List entry, TVPRA good-country listing.
3. **Documented relationship.** Customs or corporate registry evidence of a trade or ownership link.
4. **Inferred relationship.** Probabilistic network association derived from tiered trade records.
5. **Interested-party assertion.** A claim by the enterprise selling the good, whether or not an assessor has repeated it.

Tiers 3 and 4 show relationships, not conduct. Nothing at either tier supports saying a violation happened in connection with a particular good. Tier 5 sits below all others, as its source has an interest in the finding. Confidence caps follow from position in this hierarchy.

### 3.5 Aggregation and the qualification standard

The framework does not produce a composite score, for two reasons that reinforce each other.

**The first is normative.** The OECD/JRC *Handbook on Constructing Composite Indicators* establishes that any weighted aggregation treats weights as tradeoff rates, letting a surplus in one dimension offset a deficit in another.[^3] The Handbook warns against this where goals are equally legitimate and important, giving the example that economic performance should not compensate a loss in social cohesion or environmental sustainability. JRC guidance restates it: linear aggregation implies full and constant compensability.[^33] Rights do not trade against price, so an aggregation that lets a good Value score cancel a bad People and Supply Chain score writes in a substitution the Guiding Principles do not allow.

**The second is empirical.** No independent assessor in the audit covered more than three of five dimensions, and the Sayari retrieval was admissible for exactly two. Each dimension rests on different sources of different quality, so a composite would roll together evidence bases that do not overlap and hide that fact in one number. Even a reader who rejected the normative argument would face this measurement problem.

The alternative is conjunctive screening. A good qualifies only where it meets a threshold on every dimension at adequate coverage. Strong performance on one dimension cannot offset a failure on another, and **poor coverage disqualifies on its own**: a dimension nobody can assess cannot clear a threshold, so an unknown and a bad result fail the screen by different routes.

### 3.6 Audit design

Six goods were assessed against three independent review sources, both drawn from a crawl of independent review material in July 2026. Sources were profiled first; the goods are those the profiled sources had already reviewed.

The sampling is from goods with existing review attention, not at random. Evidence is more likely to exist for these goods than for an arbitrary traded good, so the absences in Table 1 are conservative. This is a most-likely-case design: the test is whether coverage holds where conditions favor it.

The three sources differ in method and trust tier: instrumented hands-on testing, desk research against regulatory and toxicological sources, and technical video analysis without a disclosed acquisition protocol. The spread tests whether coverage tracks rigor or subject matter.

Each good was coded against the source whose review surfaced it. A dimension recorded as Unknown means that the reviewer did not address it, not that no evidence exists elsewhere. Coverage was coded to the four states of §3.1 by a single coder in July 2026. The resulting distribution is reported in Table 1.

### 3.7 Source-admission testing against commercial supply-chain intelligence

The audit showed what public-facing sources cover. To test whether the gaps belong to those sources or to good-level transparency generally, one good was run against Sayari, a commercial supply-chain intelligence provider built for compliance teams.

**Case selection.** The good was one of two where the labor dimension showed total absence, so any retrieval meant movement from zero. Its parent company is publicly listed, permitting publication without the accuracy exposure attaching to findings about small private firms. Choosing a case partly on publication risk is itself a methodological constraint.

**Retrieval.** Six calls against Sayari's Model Context Protocol server in August 2026 resolved one entity from 554 candidates and returned three forced-labor risk indicators, 165 supplier relationships, and beneficial ownership resolving to the listed parent. Full parameters are in Appendix B.

All three risk indicators rested on indirect trade with a third party named in published forced-labor research, traced across tiers 2–4 within a rolling 730-day window. None recorded an observed condition at any facility. Under the §3.4 hierarchy these are tier 4, inferred relationships, and cannot support a conduct claim. Confidence was capped at Medium; only ownership reached High — confirmed independently by securities filings. The pipeline stores each indicator paired with the method that produced it, so the method sets the confidence tier and the wording shown.

**Result: two of five.** Admitted for People & Supply Chain (direct) and Transparency (derived: shipment records can test a stated country of manufacture, whether a named supplier appears in the trade record, and whether a claimed short chain is consistent with observed intermediaries). Rejected for Planet, Quality, and Value:

- **Planet.** Jurisdiction, shipping lane, and commodity codes are retrievable; emissions, water, and waste data are not. A footprint built on freight distance has no measurement behind it.
- **Quality.** Shared contract manufacturers across price tiers are visible. Common sourcing is not evidence of common durability.
- **Value.** Declared customs value against retail appears to yield a markup ratio. Declared values are shipment-aggregated, distorted by transfer pricing, and exclude tooling, warranty, and distribution. The calculation is available but would be wrong.

### 3.8 The rendering rule: fidelity is not sufficient for honesty

Two directors of the parent entity carried flags indicating association with sanctioned and forced-labor-linked entities, but only because those individuals hold directorships at other companies — the flags bear on neither the parent entity nor the good under assessment.

In theory, a faithful rendering would say something like: "this good's owner is linked to a sanctioned entity" — traceable to the source, but false in every sense an ordinary reader would take it, and possibly defamatory. In practice, the pipeline displays nothing: flags attached to people and directors are dropped where the data enters, so they never reach the record behind anything shown.

> Compliance intelligence is engineered for an analyst trained to read a flag as a lead, but a non-expert reader receives the same field as a finding. The same data, rendered faithfully, carries opposite meanings for the two audiences. Fidelity to the source is insufficient for honest disclosure and in some cases directly opposed to it.

Guiding Principle 21 requires communication on impacts with sufficient information to evaluate adequacy, without posing risks to affected stakeholders or personnel.[^5] A transparency instrument that pins rights violations on identifiable people or firms without grounds will cause harm of its own while claiming to prevent harm.

### 3.9 Quality and Value as upstream constructs

Quality meant durability and Value meant price fairness at the point of sale. Under a human rights frame those definitions do not hold: durability is not a rights construct, and retail margin fairness is not a rights question. Thus, both are redefined here as properties of buyer conduct that transmit to conditions upstream.

#### 3.9.1 The evidentiary basis

The ILO, with the Ethical Trading Initiatives, surveyed 1,454 suppliers across 87 countries. Buyer practices — price pressure, insufficient lead times, late order changes, delayed payment — bear directly on wages, working hours, and job security.[^34] Agreeing prices below production cost leaves suppliers unable to sustain decent conditions; overtime at peak periods is generated by buyers' failure to allow sufficient lead time.[^34] ILO field research across Bangladesh, China, India, South Africa and Türkiye confirms the same relationship.[^35] Anner's study of the Bangladesh garment sector reports buyer price pressure contributing to lower wages and higher production targets, and delayed buyer payment transmitting into delayed worker payment.[^36] In short, pressure on cost and specification from the buyer is a documented determinant of wages, hours, and job security at supplier level.

#### 3.9.2 Value, redefined

On March 13, 2024, the ILO Governing Body adopted the conclusions of a tripartite Meeting of Experts defining a living wage as the wage level necessary to afford a decent standard of living for workers and their families, calculated for work performed during normal hours.[^37] The concept is grounded in Article 23 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and Article 7 of the ICESCR, under which remuneration forms part of the right to just and favorable conditions of work.[^38]

In this research, the Value dimension asking whether the price paid upstream can support pay consistent with Article 7 is a rights claim, while arguing about margin is not.

**The downstream prong** asks how much of the price reaches materials and production versus brand rent and marketing. This tracks where value goes, not whether the buyer got enough of it. The two prongs are one construct observed at two points, connected by the §2.3 mechanism: where most of the price is brand rent, the premium a purchaser pays expecting to fund better conditions is not reaching the place where conditions are set. A full defense and the measurement limits are in Appendix C. A credible indicator does not yet exist, which is the gap declared in §3.9.4.

#### 3.9.3 Quality, redefined

- **Path 1: specification and cost compression.** A good's quality specification is set by the buyer, and the price and lead time attached to it are the practices the ILO survey identifies as bearing on wages and hours.[^34] A specification achieved through price compression below sustainable production cost is a determinant of conditions at supplier level.

- **Path 2: durability as an intensity denominator.** Low durability means more units for equivalent utility, each carrying the labor conditions of that chain. This is indirect and not evidenced at the individual-good level; it is a structural observation, not a finding.

#### 3.9.4 The construct: indicator gap

The redefinition changes what the dimensions mean without changing what the current indicators measure. Durability testing and price ratio do not measure purchasing practice or upstream wage adequacy, and neither decomposes price into production cost and retained rent reliably.

> In this case, Quality and Value are established as rights-relevant constructs on ILO evidence; the indicators populating them are proxies of limited fit. Constructing indicators (e.g., price-to-production-cost evidence, supplier-level wage benchmarking) that measure purchasing practice and wage adequacy directly will need further attention and work (§7).

### 4.3 Resolution capacity as the binding constraint

Substantially all inputs behind the retrieval are freely available: ownership from securities filings, bills of lading, sanctions lists, the UFLPA Entity List, the TVPRA List, and the research underlying the forced-labor indicators. What is not replicable is the join: resolving 554 name candidates to one legal entity and recursing across tiers and jurisdictions. What limits due diligence at the end of the chain is entity resolution and graph traversal, not how much gets disclosed, echoing P1.

### 4.4 Outputs

Four artifacts are published at the reference implementation, each in usable form:

- The **framework** (§3.1) is published as a reusable rubric with per-category indicators and scoring logic across twelve product types. A reader can apply it to a good this study never examined.

- The **evidence pipeline** (§4.5) is a running program that produced the records behind Table 1.

- The **point-of-purchase instrument** renders dimensional assessment where the transaction occurs, overlaying the four display states on a retailer's product page. It will be implemented as a browser extension, but the implementation is secondary: it places assessment at the moment of purchase rather than requiring a separate act of research.

- The **worked category applications** are per-category indicator sets showing how the fixed dimensions are populated differently for cookware, electronics, textiles, and others.

### 4.5 The evidence pipeline as output

The records behind Table 1 were produced by a program. The pipeline fetches a review source, profiles its methodology and monetization into a trust tier, extracts each claim typed by evidence kind, maps it to a dimension, and records every uncovered dimension as an explicit gap. Output is a set of attributed records carrying claim, evidence kind, dimension, confidence ceiling, and source URL. Extraction runs through Tavily.

Three properties distinguish it from a scrape:

- **Ingesting claims instead of verdicts.** A reviewer's overall score is stored as context and never fed to a dimension. What populates a dimension is the individual claim, so the framework does its own assessment. A claim's ceiling is set by how it was substantiated: an instrumented measurement, a third-party citation, and a repeated brand claim receive High, Medium, and Low regardless of the reviewer's verdict.
- **Treating absence as data.** An uncovered dimension is written as a gap with a note on what must be sourced independently. Table 1 is a direct read of the pipeline's output.
- **Carrying provenance and scope to the boundary.** A risk indicator is stored with the method that produced it, not as a bare boolean. Entity-level evidence is tagged at ingestion so downstream rendering carries a scope statement. Person-level flags are dropped at the adapter boundary.

The pipeline structures and attributes what a source contains; it does not verify what a source claims (i.e., a Medium-confidence record is one the pipeline correctly declined to raise, not one it checked). The Sayari adapter is a second ingestion path into the same record format, which is what enables a commercial retrieval and creator reviews to be assessed against one evidence hierarchy.

## 5. Contribution

### 5.1 The contribution stated

The evidence needed for good-level human rights due diligence exists but does not reach the end of the chain. Its raw inputs are largely public; what is proprietary is the entity resolution required to make them usable. No single source, public or commercial, covers all five dimensions at the good level.

### 5.2 A rendering standard for compliance-grade evidence

Wherever data built for experts is shown to non-experts, faithful display and honest display become separate. A layer on top of compliance-grade data cannot define its duty as fidelity to the source. It has to set a standard catering to its actual audience, and that sometimes means holding back what the source provided. An instrument that generates unfounded attribution creates an adverse impact of its own.

The adjacent cases are sustainability ratings disclosed to retail investors and algorithmic risk scoring in health and credit assessment.

### 5.3 A measurement instrument as contribution

The labeling literature compares labels against no labels, but has not tested whether the architecture of disclosure itself matters: whether separated, confidence-tiered, non-compensatory display produces different decisions than a composite. No one could run that test without a working non-compensatory instrument. This study built one, and the experiment it enables is specified in §7. In the meantime, this study does not claim disclosure architecture explains the gap between what people say and what they buy. It claims the variable has never been isolated, and that there was nothing to isolate it with until now.

The instrument is built to extend. The reviewer pipeline profiles any source through the same steps; the Sayari adapter is one ingestion path into a common record format; uncovered dimensions are recorded as gaps, so each added source fills absences rather than colliding with existing data. A verified emissions dataset would populate Planet, an independent facility-audit feed would raise People & Supply Chain from Inferred toward Scored, each entering through its own adapter at the confidence its evidence supports.

The contribution is not limited to three reviewers and one commercial retrieval: as §4 shows that no single provider covers all five dimensions, the architecture addresses this by letting new sources plug in one at a time, each through its own adapter, closing the coverage gap incrementally. What the study demonstrates is the design and two working ingestion paths. Whether it extends as built is yet to be tested, and §7 states the test.

### 5.4 Relevance to global affairs

The problem this study aims to address lies within global affairs and concerns governance, not technology. Forced labor persists due to governance failures rather than purchaser preferences. Two major markets tried to govern it and diverged (§2.5): one retreated from disclosure, the other expanded border exclusion. When the State does not settle or guarantee what a sustainability or labor claim attests, private actors fill the gap, and who gets to define those claims is a question of corporate power and information governance.

### 5.5 Relevance to policy and practice

The framework serves purchasers who are willing to attend at the point of purchase but lack research access to verify claims, and producers whose responsible practices remain invisible to buyers. In the meantime, its findings may also inform certification bodies reviewing verification requirements, consumer protection authorities preparing for enforcement of Directive (EU) 2024/825, national contact points under the OECD Guidelines, and the UN Working Group on Business and Human Rights. Specifically, responsible producers remain invisible in part because governments mandate more disclosure without making it usable: the data is published, but matching raw names and filings to actual companies across jurisdictions requires expensive tools, so only large firms benefit and smaller producers end up with no better visibility than before.

## 6. Limitations

### 6.1 The attribution problem

One problem this study does not solve is direct attribution to the individual good. Everything retrieved in §3.7 attaches to a legal entity, and reaching the good from there takes an assumption. In practice, the framework delivers entity-level evidence on its most rights-relevant dimension and labels it as such, which limits the damage but does not fix the problem.

Fixing it would need bill-of-materials disclosure or supplier-facility mapping tied to individual goods. Neither is public for most traded goods. The CSDDD originally moved toward supplier-facility mapping, but Omnibus I narrowed its scope before that requirement took hold.[^23]

### 6.2 Remaining limitations

#### Reach of the evidence

- *Sample.* Six goods is an exploratory audit, not a representative one, and the source-admission test rests on a single good.
- *One provider.* All supply-chain evidence comes from Sayari alone, untested against any other commercial source.
- *Incomplete traversal.* Sayari marked most returned paths as partial, so the visible chain ends where the data ends, not where the supply chain ends.
- *The 730-day window.* A supplier relationship that closed 25 months ago does not appear.
- *Import-record bias.* Customs data exists where jurisdictions publish it, under-representing domestic sourcing, intra-regional trade, and air freight.
- *Language and region.* Sources are overwhelmingly English-language and Western.

#### Gaps in the framework

- *Measurement gap on Quality and Value.* §3.9 grounds Quality in buyer purchasing practice and Value in upstream wage adequacy, but the available indicators measure durability and markup respectively. Closing the gap on Value needs data the study cannot reach: what production actually costs.

#### Constraints on the method

- *AI-assisted research.* AI helped survey literature and extract claims; the §3.2 checks lower the chance of an invented citation or misread source surviving, but do not eliminate it.
- *No second coder.* The framework's judgment calls follow the explicit rules of §3.1 and §3.3 and are reproducible by design, but no second coder has applied them, so replicability remains unverified. In addition, replication currently requires familiarity with the pipeline's programming and prompting across multiple interfaces, which raises the practical barrier to independent verification.

### 6.3 The causal boundary

The aim in §1.2 depends on five links: disclosure shifts demand → demand shifts market share → market share shifts production volume and therefore unit cost and distribution → price and availability move → the new conditions shift demand again. This study does not test them and addresses only the precondition for the first link, and §2.5a shows that link establishes necessity, not sufficiency. Even at the end of the chain, cheaper goods that clear their thresholds are not themselves remedy under the third pillar: remedy means the people affected actually receive something, while nothing in the chain guarantees that. An instrument that gives buyers better information while changing nothing for workers would fit every finding in this paper.

---

## 7. Conclusions and Future Research

1. **Display architecture comparison.** Whether disaggregated, confidence-tiered display alters decision quality relative to composite display. Untested in the labeling literature, and the instrument built here is the apparatus required. The hypothesis: showing a purchaser five separate dimension scores with confidence levels produces different decisions than showing them a single composite number.
2. **Extending the empirical base.** More sources, more goods, and a second commercial provider alongside Sayari, to test whether the pipeline's adapter model holds and whether any good can be found that clears the threshold on all five dimensions.
3. **Free-source replication.** §3.7 relied on Sayari, a paid service, to resolve entities and trace supply-chain relationships. Most of the raw inputs behind that retrieval — securities filings, bills of lading, sanctions lists, the UFLPA Entity List — are publicly available. The question is how much of what Sayari assembled can be rebuilt from those free sources alone. If a meaningful share can, the problem shifts from "the data is locked behind a vendor" to "this can be built as public infrastructure."
4. **Purchasing-practice indicators.** Quality and Value currently measure proxies for the rights constructs §3.9 defines. A more comprehensive set of indicators would incorporate the rights dimension, drawing on the ILO's work on purchasing practices, while also presenting tangible benefits to purchasers, so the two dimensions serve both audiences the framework addresses.
5. **How disclosure reads.** Testing whether Medium-confidence wording reaches a non-expert reader as a pattern worth investigating or as an accusation. The rendering rule currently answers that by judgment rather than evidence.

Six sequential calls against Sayari's Model Context Protocol server, 7 August 2026, traversal depth 3.[^42] Entity resolution narrowed 554 candidates to one, selected on registration identifiers, jurisdiction and trade-record volume rather than name similarity. ### Appendix C. The downstream prong of Value: defense and measurement limits (§3.9.2)

The downstream prong asks how much of the price paid is attributable to production inputs and craftsmanship rather than to brand rent and marketing expenditure. This is not the transaction-fairness question the respecification removed, and the difference lies in what is claimed rather than what is measured. Asking whether a purchaser got their money's worth is a welfare claim about one party to a sale, and no rights claim follows from it. Asking how much of the price is kept as rent instead of reaching production is a claim about where value goes along the same chain the upstream prong already examines.

The two prongs are one construct observed at two points, connected by the mechanism in §2.3. That section argues that a premium paid under conditions of weak verification accrues to the concealing actor rather than to remediation. Price decomposition is how that transfer becomes visible: where most of the price is brand rent and marketing, the premium someone pays expecting to fund better conditions is plainly not reaching the place where conditions are set. The downstream prong supplies evidence for the paper's central mechanism rather than importing a separate concern.

Two limits apply. The prong assesses where value ends up, not whether the buyer got enough of it, and no claim is made about whether a given price is justified. Measurement is hard: §3.7 rejects a markup ratio derived from declared customs value against retail price, since declared values are shipment-aggregated, distorted by transfer pricing between related entities, and exclude tooling, development, warranty and distribution.

### Appendix D. Assisted research protocol: guardrails and epistemic parallel (§3.2)

Four guardrail conditions applied to the use of AI tools in constructing this study, following the CoLab workflow:[^32]

1. Core sources were reviewed independently rather than through AI-generated summaries.
2. Every AI-surfaced citation was confirmed against the original text.
3. AI-suggested questions were treated as exploratory starting points rather than conclusions.
4. The influence of assisted systems on the study's framing is documented in the body of this paper rather than left implicit.

The verification architecture the CoLab specifies for assisted scholarship — confirming that citations point to real sources, retrieving passages to verify claims, flagging unsupported statements, and surfacing omitted perspectives[^32] — is structurally the same procedure this framework applies to corporate claims. In both cases someone is assessing assertions made by an interested party without being able to observe the underlying conditions. That parallel is deliberate: both are instances of the epistemic position a due diligence function occupies under the Guiding Principles.[^5]
