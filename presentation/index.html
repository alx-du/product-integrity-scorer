S1, S2
Lead with the fact, not the setup. Two investigations, two countries, five years apart. The consumer's designated tool for resolving ethical uncertainty is the certification label — and the certifier's own regional lead conceded that certification does not imply inspection. This is not consumers failing to care. The verification infrastructure they are instructed to trust does not reliably verify.

Two design decisions worth defending. First, the pipeline ingests claims rather than other people's scores, so you never inherit someone else's weighting. Second, when a source does not address a pillar, that absence is recorded as data rather than scored around. Most aggregation silently fills gaps. Yours flags them — and that choice is what makes the next slide possible.

S2, S4
Certification bodies exercise quasi-regulatory authority where they have no legal standing, and slide two showed the failure mode. Meanwhile mandatory disclosure is arriving from the EU and the US and propagating up chains that terminate in Asia and Latin America. This tool sits at the last mile of that regime. And the line only I can deliver — K-ESG and CSRC produce different data availability than CSRD, so confidence tiers for the same product vary by where it is made.

Contribution, Limitations, Close

A replicable, source-audited methodology for producing product-level integrity information, and documented evidence that neither certification schemes nor independent review media currently produce it.
  • LLM hallucination and overconfident scoring
  • Data availability varies by jurisdiction — confidence tiers are not comparable across origins
  • Individual purchasing is a weak lever against structural concentration
Read the contribution sentence — both legs, method and evidence. Then the limitations, unhedged. On the third one: the tool lowers search costs and applies reputational pressure at the margin. It does not fix oligopoly. Close with one sentence on why this matters for global affairs, then stop talking.
